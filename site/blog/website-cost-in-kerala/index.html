How much does a website cost in Kerala in 2026?

By Jibin Johnson Published 12 November 2026 Updated 12 November 2026 8 min read

There's no single price for a website, and anyone who quotes you one before asking about your business is guessing. A one-page site for a new café and an online store with hundreds of products are both "websites", but they're very different jobs. This guide explains what decides the website cost in Kerala in 2026, the costs people forget, and how to get a fixed quote you can trust.

The quick answer

The biggest factor is the type of site. Each step up adds pages, features and work, and the price follows the scope, not the other way round.

  • Landing page. One page with one job: bookings, sign-ups or enquiries for a single offer. The smallest scope, and often the right place to start.
  • Small business website. Home, about, services and contact, sometimes a gallery. Enough for customers to understand what you do and get in touch.
  • Business website with a blog. Everything above, plus a way to publish articles and the templates to show them. Worth it if you plan to bring in visitors through search.
  • E-commerce website. Products, cart, payments, delivery options, stock and order emails. The largest scope, and the one with the most work after launch.

Two sites of the same type can still differ a lot in price. The rest of this guide is about why.

What affects the price

Number of pages and templates

Ten pages that share one layout take less work than ten pages that each need their own. Developers think in templates: a service page, a blog post, a contact page. The number of unique templates matters more than the raw page count.

Custom design or a template

A ready-made theme is quicker and cheaper upfront, but your site will look like many others and you'll keep working around its limits. A custom design is planned around your business, your customers and your content. It takes longer because someone is actually designing it.

Animation and interaction

Scroll effects, animated product stories and custom interactions take real time to build well, and more time to keep them fast on phones. Done badly, they slow the site down. Ask what each effect is for before paying for it.

Content writing and photos

Someone has to write every word on the site. If you supply finished text and good photos, the job is smaller. If you need copywriting, product photos or a short shoot at your premises, that's extra work, and it's often what delays a project most.

SEO setup

A site can launch with no thought for search, or with the foundations in place: unique page titles and descriptions, a clean heading structure, schema markup, a sitemap, fast loading and a Google Business Profile that points to it. Getting this right during the build costs far less than fixing it later.

Features and integrations

Booking systems, payment gateways like Razorpay, WhatsApp chat, forms that feed a CRM, member logins, and Malayalam and English versions of every page all add work. Some come with their own subscriptions too. List what you need on day one and what can wait.

Freelancer vs agency vs DIY builders

There's no wrong choice here, only a wrong fit. Each option suits a different kind of project.

Freelancer

You talk directly to the person building your site, decisions are quick and overheads are lower. The trade-off is capacity: one person can only take on so much, so a good freelancer may have a waiting list, and you depend on them being available. Look at their past work, and ask what happens if they're away when something breaks.

Agency

An agency brings a team: designers, developers, writers and project managers. That suits large sites, tight deadlines and businesses that want one company to handle everything. You pay for that team and the process around it, and you may never speak to the people doing the work.

DIY website builders

Wix, Squarespace and similar tools let you build a basic site yourself, and for a very small business that's sometimes enough. The cost is your time, a subscription that never ends, and limits on speed, SEO and design that show up once you want to grow. Moving away later usually means starting again.

Costs people forget

Building the site is a one-time cost. Running it isn't. Ask about each of these before you agree a price:

  • Domain name. Renewed every year. Make sure it's registered in your name, not the developer's.
  • Hosting. Where the site lives. A simple, fast site is cheap to host; WordPress and online stores need more.
  • Business email. An address at your own domain usually has its own yearly plan.
  • Maintenance. Software updates, security patches, backups and fixing things that break. WordPress sites need this regularly because of their plugins.
  • Content updates. New services, a new menu, staff changes. Find out whether you can make these yourself or pay for each one.
  • Ongoing SEO. Good foundations at launch are a start, but ranking in a competitive market takes continued work.
  • Paid plugins, themes and tools. Many have yearly licences that are easy to miss in a quote.

Cheap websites that cost more later

A low quote isn't a problem in itself. The problem is what gets left out to reach it. These are the issues I see most often when a business comes to me for a rebuild:

  • Slow loading. Heavy themes, oversized images and too many plugins make pages slow, especially on mobile data. Visitors leave before the page appears, and Google notices.
  • No SEO foundations. Missing titles, the same description on every page, no schema and no sitemap. The site exists, but nobody finds it.
  • Hard to edit. If changing a phone number needs a developer, every small update costs you time and money.
  • You don't own it. The domain, hosting or code sits in someone else's account, and getting it back is slow or impossible.
  • The rebuild. When a site can't be fixed, you pay for a second website. That's the most expensive outcome of all.

Paying more doesn't guarantee quality either. What protects you is knowing exactly what's included.

How to get an accurate quote

The clearer your brief, the more accurate the quote, and the fewer surprises later. Before you contact a developer, prepare:

  1. What the site is for. More enquiries, bookings, online sales or credibility with investors. One main goal beats five.
  2. Who it's for. Your customers, where they are and what they search for.
  3. The pages you think you need. A rough list is fine.
  4. Features. Booking, payments, forms, languages, a blog.
  5. Content. Whether you'll provide the text and photos or need help with them.
  6. Examples. Two or three sites you like, and what you like about each.
  7. Your timeline. Including any date the site must be live for, like a launch or an event.

Then ask every developer the same questions: what's included, what isn't, who owns the domain and the code, what happens after launch, and how changes are charged. A good developer will ask you questions too. If someone quotes without asking anything, they're quoting for a site they haven't understood.

FAQ

Why don't you list website prices on your site?

Because a fair price depends on your scope, and a number without context sets the wrong expectations. Two businesses asking for a five-page website can need very different things. After a short call, I send a fixed quote that lists exactly what's included.

How long does it take to build a business website?

It depends on the size of the site and how quickly the content is ready. A landing page moves faster than a full business site, and an online store takes longest. Waiting for text and photos is the most common cause of delay, so preparing them early saves the most time.

Is WordPress cheaper than a hand-coded website?

Sometimes upfront, not always over time. WordPress needs regular updates, security care and often paid plugins. A hand-coded site has less to maintain and is usually faster, but changes may need a developer unless simple editing is set up. The right choice depends on how often you'll update the site.

Will I have to pay again every year?

Yes, for a few things. Domain renewal, hosting and business email are yearly costs for almost every website. Maintenance, paid plugins and ongoing SEO depend on your site and your goals. Ask for these to be listed separately in your quote.

Should I choose a fixed quote or an hourly rate?

For a website build, a fixed quote is usually easier to plan around, as long as the scope is written down clearly. Hourly billing suits ongoing work like maintenance or small changes, where the amount of work varies from month to month.
